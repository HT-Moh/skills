# Validate Startup Ideas

Test behavior, not stated interest. Search public first-hand evidence, then say what it proves and what it does not.

## Define the test

Ask only for missing details that change the search: the idea in one sentence; customer segment, role, location or language; problem and desired outcome; buyer; current alternatives; and market limits.

Test these assumptions:

1. The customer has the problem often enough to care.
2. It costs time, money, risk, lost sales, or stress.
3. They use, build, or pay for a workaround.
4. The offer is meaningfully better than the current option.
5. A reachable group of these customers exists.

Do not ask whether people like the idea. Find proof of the first four assumptions.

## Find first-hand evidence

Use available search and browsing tools. Search several relevant sources:

- Niche forums and professional communities.
- Reddit and public social posts.
- Product and app reviews.
- Public issue trackers, support forums, and feature requests.
- Blog comments, public videos, podcasts, and Q&A.
- Job posts when the problem creates a real work need.

Search in the customer language. Combine problem terms with action terms: `frustrated`, `manual`, `spreadsheet`, `workaround`, `alternative`, `switch`, `cancelled`, `expensive`, `hours`, `help`, `recommend`, `looking for`, and `how do I`.

Search alternatives by name plus complaint terms. Search for the moment the problem occurs, not just the solution category. For example, use `late invoice follow up manual` before `invoice AI`.

Keep only sources with a working link, date when available, and a clear first-hand statement. Prefer recent posts but retain older evidence when the problem is stable. Do not count copied articles, trend pieces, or promotion as customer proof.

## Score evidence

For each item record customer context, problem, impact, current action, spend signal, timing, platform, date, link, and at most one short exact phrase. Paraphrase the rest. Count copied claims once.

Give each item 0 to 2 points:

| Factor | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-hand experience | Opinion or repost | Vague personal mention | Clear direct experience |
| Pain | No stated cost | Minor delay | Serious time, money, risk, or lost result |
| Current action | No action | Looking for ideas | Uses, builds, changes, or pays for a fix |
| Recency | Old or missing date | Within two years | Within six months or ongoing |
| Customer fit | Outside target | Partial fit | Direct target customer |

- **Strong:** 8 to 10. Interview candidate and demand proof.
- **Useful:** 5 to 7. Supports a pattern but needs more proof.
- **Weak:** 0 to 4. Do not use to claim demand.

Judge the full set:

- **Strong signal:** five or more independent strong items across two or more sources, including current action or spending.
- **Mixed signal:** clear pain but weak action, or proof from one narrow group.
- **Weak signal:** mainly opinions, future wishes, or unclear customer fit.

State the independent-source count. Never turn a small sample into a market-size claim.

## Find interview targets

Recommend people by observable behavior, not title alone. Prioritize people who recently described the problem, asked for help, changed tools, built a manual process, hired help, paid for a current fix, or suffered a related failed result.

Give every target group a recruiting route: a public community, search query, review site, professional group, or customer list supplied by the user. Link only public posts or public contact routes. Do not collect private details, bypass privacy controls, or suggest spam.

Rank groups by urgency, fit, ability to buy, and ease of contact.

## Run problem interviews

Ask about a recent event:

1. Tell me about the last time this happened.
2. What triggered it?
3. What did you do next?
4. What tools, people, or manual steps did you use?
5. What did it cost in time, money, or missed results?
6. What have you tried before, and why was it not enough?
7. Who else approves or buys a fix?
8. What would need to change for you to switch or pay?

Do not lead with a demo, ask if they would buy, or seek praise. Ask permission before recording or reusing words. Write a short learning-focused outreach note, under 15 minutes, with an easy way to decline.

## Pick the next test

| Missing proof | Next test |
|---|---|
| Frequent painful problem | Five to ten problem interviews |
| Desire for a better result | Problem-focused landing page with interview follow-up |
| Money or effort commitment | Price test, deposit, paid pilot, or letter of intent |
| Ability to deliver | Concierge service or manual prototype |
| Ability to reach customers | Small outreach test in the best source community |

Favor deposits, pilots, repeated use, and referrals over clicks, survey votes, or compliments. Define the pass condition first.

## Report format

Return:

1. **Verdict:** strong, mixed, or weak and why.
2. **Customer experience:** three to five evidence-backed patterns.
3. **Evidence table:** source, customer context, problem, current action, score, and link.
4. **Interview targets:** ranked groups, recruiting routes, and reasons.
5. **Interview guide:** questions and outreach note.
6. **Next test:** one test, pass condition, and what result changes the idea.
7. **Limits:** missing groups, source bias, and untested assumptions.

If evidence is thin, say so. Recommend a narrower problem, a different customer group, or direct interviews. Never invent evidence, sources, interviewees, or demand.
